# Analytics and Performance — Quick Revision Notes

**Main idea:** define the metric's population, grain, time window, and denominator before calculating it. Then make the query efficient without changing that meaning.

Sources: [Advanced SQL questions](<../Source Materials/Advanced_SQL_100_Query_Questions_Vol1 (2).pdf>) and [PrepNPlaced interview questions](<../Source Materials/Updated_100_SQL_Interview_Questions.pdf>). These tiny examples are invented to explain concepts; they do not answer a source practice question.

## 1. Start with one row per business event

| customer_id | signup_month | order_month | order_amount |
|---:|---|---|---:|
| 1 | 2026-01 | 2026-01 | 40 |
| 1 | 2026-01 | 2026-02 | 20 |
| 2 | 2026-01 | 2026-01 | 30 |
| 3 | 2026-02 | 2026-02 | 50 |

One row here is an order. Counting rows gives four orders, but COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id) gives three customers. Say which entity a metric counts before writing SQL.

| calculation | January | February |
|---|---:|---:|
| Order value | 40 + 30 = 70 | 20 + 50 = 70 |
| Distinct buyers | 2 | 2 |

The same monthly value of 70 hides a change in who ordered. A KPI label must name both its grain and its unit: currency value, orders, or customers.

## 2. Cohorts and retention use customers as the denominator

A cohort is a group that entered at the same starting time. In this example, customers 1 and 2 signed up in January. Of those two, customer 1 placed an order in February.

| cohort | eligible customers | customers active next month | next-month retention |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 2026-01 | 2 | 1 | 1 / 2 = 50% |

There are two January orders, but the denominator is **two customers**, not two orders. If a customer made five February orders, they would still count as one retained customer. Define whether “active” means placed, paid, delivered, or some other business event.

A cohort formed near the end of the available data may not have a full next-month observation window. Mark it immature or exclude it instead of treating “not yet observable” as zero retention.

## 3. Cumulative value and concentration

For customer 1, cumulative order value is 40 after January and 40 + 20 = 60 after February. A running total needs a meaningful order: month first, then a deterministic tiebreaker if several rows share a month.

A separate three-product example shows contribution to a whole:

| product | value | share of 100 | cumulative share after sorting high to low |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 60 | 60% | 60% |
| B | 30 | 30% | 90% |
| C | 10 | 10% | 100% |

The 80% threshold is crossed **after B**, even though no row is exactly 80%. State whether a “top 20%” requirement means 20% of entities by count or entities that contribute most of the value; those are different questions.

## 4. Basket metrics have different denominators

In four invented orders, A appears in three, B appears in three, and both appear together in two.

| metric for A and B | calculation | result |
|---|---|---:|
| Support of A and B | 2 orders with both / 4 total orders | 50% |
| Confidence of A to B | 2 orders with both / 3 orders with A | 66.67% |
| Lift of A to B | 66.67% / 75% support of B | 0.89 |

Support, confidence, and lift answer different questions. Build one distinct order-product pair before counting baskets; duplicate lines for the same product in one order should not count as extra baskets.

## 5. Performance checks that preserve the answer

| Check | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| State the final grain | A join can accidentally multiply metric rows |
| Filter with a raw date range | An indexed date column is easier to search than a function wrapped around it |
| Aggregate each one-to-many source first | Prevents header totals from being repeated by detail rows |
| Use an index that matches filters and joins | Reduces work when the plan can use it |
| Inspect the execution plan and row counts | Reveals scans, row multiplication, and expensive sorts |
| Recheck the output after tuning | A faster query is useful only if it returns the same rows and totals |

For example, a query that filters orders by cust_id and then an order_date range may benefit from an index beginning with cust_id followed by order_date. Confirm with the actual plan and data; an index is not a promise that the optimizer will use it.

A CTE can make stages easier to read, but it is not automatically materialized. An optimizer may merge it, and a correlated subquery may be rewritten. Measure plans rather than guessing from SQL appearance alone.

## 6. Final metric checklist

1. What is one input row, one intermediate row, and one output row?
2. Who is included or excluded from the population?
3. What event and time zone define each date bucket?
4. What is the numerator and denominator? Can either be zero or unknown?
5. Are incomplete current periods comparable with complete past periods?
6. Do duplicates, missing relationships, or several child tables change the count?
7. After tuning, did the rows and totals remain identical?
