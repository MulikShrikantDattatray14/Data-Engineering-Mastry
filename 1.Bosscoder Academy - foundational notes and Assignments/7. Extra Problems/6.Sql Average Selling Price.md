Table: `Prices`

| Column Name | Type |
|---|---|
| product_id | int |
| start_date | date |
| end_date | date |
| price | int |

(product_id, start_date, end_date) is the primary key (combination of columns with unique values) for this table.

Each row of this table indicates the price of the product_id in the period from start_date to end_date.

For each product_id there will be no two overlapping periods. That means there will be no two intersecting periods for the same product_id.

Table: `UnitsSold`

| Column Name | Type |
|---|---|
| product_id | int |
| purchase_date | date |
| units | int |

This table may contain duplicate rows.

Each row of this table indicates the date, units, and product_id of each product sold.

Write a solution to find the average selling price for each product. `average_price` should be **rounded to 2 decimal places**. If a product does not have any sold units, its average selling price is assumed to be 0.

Return the result table in **any order**.

The result format is in the following example.

**Example 1:**

**Input:**

Prices table:

| product_id | start_date | end_date | price |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2019-02-17 | 2019-02-28 | 5 |
| 1 | 2019-03-01 | 2019-03-22 | 20 |
| 2 | 2019-02-01 | 2019-02-20 | 15 |
| 2 | 2019-02-21 | 2019-03-31 | 30 |

UnitsSold table:

| product_id | purchase_date | units |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2019-02-25 | 100 |
| 1 | 2019-03-01 | 15 |
| 2 | 2019-02-10 | 200 |
| 2 | 2019-03-22 | 30 |

**Output:**

| product_id | average_price |
|---|---|
| 1 | 6.96 |
| 2 | 16.96 |

**Explanation:**

Average selling price = Total Price of Product / Number of products sold.

Average selling price for product 1 = ((100 \* 5) + (15 \* 20)) / 115 = 6.96

Average selling price for product 2 = ((200 \* 15) + (30 \* 30)) / 230 = 16.96

## My solution — SQLite

```sql
SELECT
    P.product_id,
    COALESCE(
        ROUND(
            SUM(P.price * U.units) * 1.0 / SUM(U.units),
            2
        ),
        0
    ) AS average_price
FROM Prices AS P
LEFT JOIN UnitsSold AS U
    ON P.product_id = U.product_id
    AND U.purchase_date >= P.start_date
    AND U.purchase_date <= P.end_date
GROUP BY P.product_id;
```

### Step-by-step explanation with one example

The join must match **the same product** and **the price that was valid on the purchase date**. Matching only `product_id` cannot tell us which price applies.

Follow the same supplied example through every step.

**1. Understand what each table tells us**

`Prices`:

| product_id | start_date | end_date | price |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2019-02-17 | 2019-02-28 | 5 |
| 1 | 2019-03-01 | 2019-03-22 | 20 |
| 2 | 2019-02-01 | 2019-02-20 | 15 |
| 2 | 2019-02-21 | 2019-03-31 | 30 |

Each row answers:

> What was this product's price during this period?

For product `1`, the price was:

- `5` from February 17 through February 28.
- `20` from March 1 through March 22.

`UnitsSold`:

| product_id | purchase_date | units |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2019-02-25 | 100 |
| 1 | 2019-03-01 | 15 |
| 2 | 2019-02-10 | 200 |
| 2 | 2019-03-22 | 30 |

Each row answers:

> How many units of this product were sold on this date?

For example, product `1` sold **100 units on February 25**. To calculate the value of that sale, we need to find its price **on February 25**.

**2. See what happens when we join only on product_id**

Consider this incomplete join:

```sql
SELECT
    P.product_id,
    P.start_date,
    P.end_date,
    P.price,
    U.purchase_date,
    U.units
FROM Prices AS P
LEFT JOIN UnitsSold AS U
    ON P.product_id = U.product_id
ORDER BY P.product_id, P.start_date, U.purchase_date;
```

Result:

| product_id | start_date | end_date | price | purchase_date | units |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2019-02-17 | 2019-02-28 | 5 | 2019-02-25 | 100 |
| 1 | 2019-02-17 | 2019-02-28 | 5 | 2019-03-01 | 15 |
| 1 | 2019-03-01 | 2019-03-22 | 20 | 2019-02-25 | 100 |
| 1 | 2019-03-01 | 2019-03-22 | 20 | 2019-03-01 | 15 |
| 2 | 2019-02-01 | 2019-02-20 | 15 | 2019-02-10 | 200 |
| 2 | 2019-02-01 | 2019-02-20 | 15 | 2019-03-22 | 30 |
| 2 | 2019-02-21 | 2019-03-31 | 30 | 2019-02-10 | 200 |
| 2 | 2019-02-21 | 2019-03-31 | 30 | 2019-03-22 | 30 |

Why eight rows? Each product has **two price periods and two sales rows**. Matching the product ID alone connects each price period to both sales:

```text
2 price periods * 2 sales rows = 4 combinations per product
```

Look at product `1`:

- The February 25 sale is attached to both price `5` and price `20`.
- The March 1 sale is also attached to both prices.

The product IDs match, but **some prices are wrong for the purchase date**.

**3. Add the date conditions to choose the correct price**

A valid match must satisfy all three conditions:

```sql
P.product_id = U.product_id
AND U.purchase_date >= P.start_date
AND U.purchase_date <= P.end_date
```

Read that as:

> Same product, and the purchase happened within this price period.

Trace the **February 25 sale of 100 units of product 1**:

| Possible price | Purchase on/after start? | Purchase on/before end? | Valid match? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5, valid February 17-28 | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| 20, valid March 1-22 | No | Yes | No |

February 25 is before March 1, so the price-`20` row must not match that sale.

For the **March 1 sale of 15 units**:

- It is outside the February period because March 1 is after February 28.
- It is inside the March period because March 1 is equal to its start date.

That is why we use **`>=` and `<=`**: purchases on either boundary date are included.

Here is the complete matching query:

```sql
SELECT
    P.product_id,
    P.start_date,
    P.end_date,
    P.price,
    U.purchase_date,
    U.units
FROM Prices AS P
LEFT JOIN UnitsSold AS U
    ON P.product_id = U.product_id
    AND U.purchase_date >= P.start_date
    AND U.purchase_date <= P.end_date
ORDER BY P.product_id, P.start_date, U.purchase_date;
```

Result:

| product_id | start_date | end_date | price | purchase_date | units |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2019-02-17 | 2019-02-28 | 5 | 2019-02-25 | 100 |
| 1 | 2019-03-01 | 2019-03-22 | 20 | 2019-03-01 | 15 |
| 2 | 2019-02-01 | 2019-02-20 | 15 | 2019-02-10 | 200 |
| 2 | 2019-02-21 | 2019-03-31 | 30 | 2019-03-22 | 30 |

**The eight combinations have become four correct matches.**

The question guarantees that price periods do not overlap for a product. Therefore, a purchase can match at most one price period. All four purchases in this example have a matching period.

**4. Understand why the conditions belong in ON**

The requirement includes products without sales, so we use:

```sql
FROM Prices AS P
LEFT JOIN UnitsSold AS U
```

A `LEFT JOIN` preserves each `Prices` row even when no sale satisfies the matching conditions. In that case, the `U` columns contain NULL.

The distinction is:

| Clause | What it controls |
|---|---|
| ON | Which sales match a price row |
| WHERE | Which rows remain after the join |

If the date conditions were in `WHERE`, an unmatched row would have a NULL `U.purchase_date`. That NULL cannot satisfy either date comparison, so the row would be removed.

Keeping the date conditions in **`ON`** lets SQL say:

> No sale matches this price period, but preserve the price row because this is a LEFT JOIN.

Every price period in this sample has a matching sale, so the current result has no unmatched rows. In other inputs, a product can have sales in one price period and no sales in another. LEFT JOIN also preserves products with no matching sales in any period.

**5. Calculate the value of each matched sale**

Now that each sale has the correct price, calculate:

```sql
P.price * U.units
```

Query:

```sql
SELECT
    P.product_id,
    U.purchase_date,
    P.price,
    U.units,
    P.price * U.units AS sale_value
FROM Prices AS P
LEFT JOIN UnitsSold AS U
    ON P.product_id = U.product_id
    AND U.purchase_date >= P.start_date
    AND U.purchase_date <= P.end_date
ORDER BY P.product_id, U.purchase_date;
```

Result:

| product_id | purchase_date | price | units | sale_value |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2019-02-25 | 5 | 100 | 500 |
| 1 | 2019-03-01 | 20 | 15 | 300 |
| 2 | 2019-02-10 | 15 | 200 | 3000 |
| 2 | 2019-03-22 | 30 | 30 | 900 |

For product `1`:

```text
100 units * price 5 = 500
15 units * price 20 = 300
```

Its total sales value is therefore `800`.

**6. Group by product and calculate the weighted average**

We need one answer per product, so use:

```sql
GROUP BY P.product_id
```

The calculation is:

```text
average selling price = total sales value / total units sold
```

In SQL:

```sql
SUM(P.price * U.units) * 1.0 / SUM(U.units)
```

Inspect the totals and average:

```sql
SELECT
    P.product_id,
    SUM(P.price * U.units) AS total_sales_value,
    SUM(U.units) AS total_units,
    SUM(P.price * U.units) * 1.0
        / SUM(U.units) AS unrounded_average
FROM Prices AS P
LEFT JOIN UnitsSold AS U
    ON P.product_id = U.product_id
    AND U.purchase_date >= P.start_date
    AND U.purchase_date <= P.end_date
GROUP BY P.product_id;
```

Result, abbreviating the decimal expansion:

| product_id | total_sales_value | total_units | unrounded_average |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 800 | 115 | 6.956521... |
| 2 | 3900 | 230 | 16.956521... |

Manual calculations:

```text
Product 1:
(500 + 300) / (100 + 15)
= 800 / 115
= 6.956521...

Product 2:
(3000 + 900) / (200 + 30)
= 3900 / 230
= 16.956521...
```

The number of units matters: selling **100 units at price 5** contributes more weight than selling **15 units at price 20**.

The `1.0` ensures decimal division in SQLite. Without it, integer division would turn `800 / 115` into `6` before rounding.

**7. Round the average and handle products without sales**

First, round the calculated average:

```sql
ROUND(
    SUM(P.price * U.units) * 1.0 / SUM(U.units),
    2
)
```

For this sample:

| product_id | Before rounding | After rounding |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6.956521... | 6.96 |
| 2 | 16.956521... | 16.96 |

For a product without matching sales, the sums and calculated average are NULL. The question requires zero, so wrap the final calculation in:

```sql
COALESCE(calculated_average, 0)
```

This is why `COALESCE` belongs around the **result**. Replacing only the denominator with zero would not produce the required average.

**8. Assemble the final query**

```sql
SELECT
    P.product_id,
    COALESCE(
        ROUND(
            SUM(P.price * U.units) * 1.0 / SUM(U.units),
            2
        ),
        0
    ) AS average_price
FROM Prices AS P
LEFT JOIN UnitsSold AS U
    ON P.product_id = U.product_id
    AND U.purchase_date >= P.start_date
    AND U.purchase_date <= P.end_date
GROUP BY P.product_id;
```

Final output:

| product_id | average_price |
|---|---|
| 1 | 6.96 |
| 2 | 16.96 |

The intermediate queries and final query were executed in SQLite. The displayed matches, totals, and final averages agree with the supplied example.

## My solution — SQL Server

The join and weighted-average logic are preserved. NULLIF(SUM(U.units), 0) converts a zero denominator to NULL so COALESCE can return 0 without a SQL Server division-by-zero error. This preserves the SQLite result for a zero total of units.

```sql
SELECT
    P.product_id,
    COALESCE(
        ROUND(
            SUM(P.price * U.units) * 1.0 / NULLIF(SUM(U.units), 0),
            2
        ),
        0
    ) AS average_price
FROM Prices AS P
LEFT JOIN UnitsSold AS U
    ON P.product_id = U.product_id
    AND U.purchase_date >= P.start_date
    AND U.purchase_date <= P.end_date
GROUP BY P.product_id;
```
