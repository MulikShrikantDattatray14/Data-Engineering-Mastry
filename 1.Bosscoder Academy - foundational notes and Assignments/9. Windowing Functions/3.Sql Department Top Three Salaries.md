# 3. SQL Department Top Three Salaries

## Question and image

[**185. Department Top Three Salaries**](https://leetcode.com/problems/department-top-three-salaries/)

Find the employees whose salaries are among the **three highest distinct salary values** in their department. Include every employee tied at a qualifying salary.

Return `Department`, `Employee`, and `Salary`, ordered by `Department` ascending, then `Salary` descending, then `Employee` ascending.

**Example 1 — input:**

Employee:

| id | name | salary | departmentId |
|---:|---|---:|---:|
| 1 | Joe | 85000 | 1 |
| 2 | Henry | 80000 | 2 |
| 3 | Sam | 60000 | 2 |
| 4 | Max | 90000 | 1 |
| 5 | Janet | 69000 | 1 |
| 6 | Randy | 85000 | 1 |
| 7 | Will | 70000 | 1 |

Department:

| id | name |
|---:|---|
| 1 | IT |
| 2 | Sales |

**Expected output:**

| Department | Employee | Salary |
|---|---|---:|
| IT | Max | 90000 |
| IT | Joe | 85000 |
| IT | Randy | 85000 |
| IT | Will | 70000 |
| Sales | Henry | 80000 |
| Sales | Sam | 60000 |

**Explanation:** In IT, the three highest distinct salaries are 90,000 (Max), 85,000 (Joe and Randy), and 70,000 (Will). Janet's 69,000 is fourth and is excluded. In Sales, Henry earns 80,000 and Sam earns 60,000; there is no third distinct salary. Joe appears before Randy because their salaries tie and `Employee` is sorted ascending.

## My solution — SQLite

Your final query ranks distinct salary levels within each department, then keeps the first three levels.

```sql
WITH ranked_employees AS (
    SELECT
        D.name AS Department,
        E.name AS Employee,
        E.salary AS Salary,
        DENSE_RANK() OVER (
            PARTITION BY D.id
            ORDER BY E.salary DESC
        ) AS salary_rank
    FROM Employee AS E
    JOIN Department AS D
        ON E.departmentId = D.id
)
SELECT Department, Employee, Salary
FROM ranked_employees
WHERE salary_rank <= 3
ORDER BY Department ASC, Salary DESC, Employee ASC;
```

### Step-by-step explanation with one example

**1. Start with the two tables.** The supplied `Employee` rows identify an employee, salary, and department ID; `Department` provides the department name. The same sample is used in every step:

Employee:

| id | name | salary | departmentId |
| ---: | --- | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | Joe | 85000 | 1 |
| 2 | Henry | 80000 | 2 |
| 3 | Sam | 60000 | 2 |
| 4 | Max | 90000 | 1 |
| 5 | Janet | 69000 | 1 |
| 6 | Randy | 85000 | 1 |
| 7 | Will | 70000 | 1 |

Department:

| id | name |
| ---: | --- |
| 1 | IT |
| 2 | Sales |

**2. Join each employee to their department.** `E.departmentId = D.id` matches each employee to the department name needed in the final output. The following query displays the joined rows in employee ID order for inspection; its `ORDER BY` affects only this displayed intermediate result.

```sql
SELECT
    E.id AS employee_id,
    E.name AS employee_name,
    E.salary,
    E.departmentId AS department_id,
    D.name AS department_name
FROM Employee AS E
JOIN Department AS D
    ON E.departmentId = D.id
ORDER BY E.id;
```

| employee_id | employee_name | salary | department_id | department_name |
| ---: | --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| 1 | Joe | 85000 | 1 | IT |
| 2 | Henry | 80000 | 2 | Sales |
| 3 | Sam | 60000 | 2 | Sales |
| 4 | Max | 90000 | 1 | IT |
| 5 | Janet | 69000 | 1 | IT |
| 6 | Randy | 85000 | 1 | IT |
| 7 | Will | 70000 | 1 | IT |

All seven employees remain after this inner join. `E.name` and `D.name` are given different aliases so the employee and department names are not confused.

**3. Rank salary levels separately in each department.** `PARTITION BY D.id` restarts the ranking for every department; the unique ID prevents two same-named departments from being mixed. `ORDER BY E.salary DESC` puts higher salaries first. `DENSE_RANK()` gives tied salaries the same rank and does not skip the next rank.

```sql
SELECT
    D.id AS department_id,
    D.name AS department_name,
    E.name AS employee_name,
    E.salary,
    DENSE_RANK() OVER (
        PARTITION BY D.id
        ORDER BY E.salary DESC
    ) AS salary_rank
FROM Employee AS E
JOIN Department AS D
    ON E.departmentId = D.id
ORDER BY D.name ASC, E.salary DESC, E.name ASC;
```

| department_id | department_name | employee_name | salary | salary_rank |
| ---: | --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | IT | Max | 90000 | 1 |
| 1 | IT | Joe | 85000 | 2 |
| 1 | IT | Randy | 85000 | 2 |
| 1 | IT | Will | 70000 | 3 |
| 1 | IT | Janet | 69000 | 4 |
| 2 | Sales | Henry | 80000 | 1 |
| 2 | Sales | Sam | 60000 | 2 |

Joe and Randy both earn 85,000, so both get rank 2. Will's 70,000 is the third **distinct** salary and gets rank 3. `RANK()` would skip rank 3 after Joe and Randy's tie, while `ROW_NUMBER()` would give them different positions; neither expresses the required top three distinct salary levels.

**4. Filter the ranks and return the requested columns.** The window result is named `salary_rank` inside the CTE. The outer query can then use `WHERE salary_rank <= 3`, dropping Janet (rank 4) while retaining every employee tied at a qualifying rank. Finally, select only `Department`, `Employee`, and `Salary`, and sort by department name ascending, salary descending, and employee name ascending.

| Department | Employee | Salary |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| IT | Max | 90000 |
| IT | Joe | 85000 |
| IT | Randy | 85000 |
| IT | Will | 70000 |
| Sales | Henry | 80000 |
| Sales | Sam | 60000 |

**Manual check:** IT's distinct salaries are 90,000, 85,000, 70,000, and 69,000. The first three include Max, Joe, Randy, and Will. Sales has only two distinct salaries, so both employees qualify. The complete query above produced these six rows on the sample in SQLite.
